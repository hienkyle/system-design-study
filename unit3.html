SYSTEM DESIGN PRIMER · UNIT 3

Latency vs throughput

The second of the primer’s three high-level trade-offs. Latency describes how long one piece of work takes; throughput describes how many pieces of work get done in a given time. They measure the same process from two different angles, and a design target needs both.[1][2]

Primer section · Latency vs throughputCadence · “Understanding Latency versus Throughput” (2022)

3.1Two definitions

The two terms are often confused or used as if they meant the same thing.[2] The primer defines them in one line each:[1]

Latency

The time to perform some action or to produce some result.

Throughput

The number of such actions or results per unit of time.

Units of each measure

Because one is a duration and the other is a rate, they carry different units. Latency is measured in time: hours, seconds, nanoseconds, or, in hardware, clock periods (one tick of the chip’s clock). Throughput is measured in whatever is being produced, divided by time: cars, I/O samples, memory words or loop iterations per second, hour or clock period. For memory systems, throughput is sometimes called memory bandwidth.[2]

In Figure 1, each bar is one request. The length of a single bar is its latency. Throughput ignores how long any one bar is and simply counts how many bars finish inside a fixed window of time. Because work can overlap, the two numbers do not have to be reciprocals of each other.

3.2The car factory

Cadence’s example: an assembly line takes 8 hours to build one car, and the factory turns out 120 cars per day. The latency is 8 hours. The throughput is 120 cars/day, which is 120 ÷ 24 = 5 cars/hour.[2] Both describe the same factory; neither one can be worked out from the other alone.

Table 2. The car factory in numbers
MeasureValueHow it is read
Latency8 hoursTime for one car to go from start to finished
Throughput120 cars/day = 5 cars/hourFinished cars leaving the factory per unit of time

Why both numbers hold

If the line built one car at a time, an 8-hour latency would cap output at 1 ÷ 8 = 0.125 cars/hour. Getting 5 cars/hour means many cars are on the line at once, each at a different stage, so a finished car leaves every 12 minutes even though each one took 8 hours. Little’s law gives the size of that overlap: the average number of items in a system equals the rate at which they leave (throughput) times the time each spends inside (latency).[3] Here that is 5 cars/h × 8 h = about 40 cars in progress.

Takeaway

Throughput can rise without latency falling: add more work in parallel (more cars on the line) and output goes up while each car still takes 8 hours.

3.3The design goal

The primer’s guideline is short: aim for maximal throughput with acceptable latency.[1] Throughput is the quantity to push as high as possible, because it decides how much total work the system can absorb. Latency is a constraint: it only has to stay inside a limit set by the requirements, and the limit is what “acceptable” means.

The hardware example in 3.4 shows the shape of such a requirement. The spec states a latency budget (computation must finish within 1000 ns) and a throughput the device must reach (640 Mbit/s), and the design must satisfy both.[2]

Where this fits

3.4Worked example: a hardware spec

Cadence writes for hardware engineers who now share C++/SystemC code and vocabulary with system designers through High-Level Synthesis (HLS), a flow that generates hardware from such high-level code.[2] Its second example is a communications device with these requirements:

Table 3. Requirements for the communications device
RequirementValueWhich measure
Clock frequency100 MHzSets the size of one clock period
Available computation time1000 nsLatency budget
Device throughput640 Mbit/sThroughput
Output word width64 bitsSize of one result

Converting the spec

A hardware designer works in clock periods, so both figures are converted into that unit:[2]

  1. Clock period. At 100 MHz the clock ticks 100 million times per second, so one period lasts 10 ns.1 ÷ 100 MHz = 10 ns
  2. Latency in clock periods. The 1000 ns budget fits 100 periods.1000 ns ÷ 10 ns = 100 clock periods
  3. Throughput in words. Each result is one 64-bit word.640 Mbit/s ÷ 64 bit = 10 million words/s
  4. Throughput per clock period. Divide by the 100 million periods in each second.10 M words/s ÷ 100 M periods/s = 0.1 words/periodThat is the same as one word every 10 clock periods.

These two values, a latency of 100 clock periods and 10 periods between output words, are what the designer builds the hardware to meet.[2] Figure 3 draws them on one timeline: outputs leave every 10 periods while each one took 100 periods to produce, so roughly 100 ÷ 10 = 10 words are in progress at once, the same overlap seen in the car factory.[3]

The “throughput = 10” shorthand

Watch the units

Some tools report throughput as the number of clock periods between outputs. For this device such a tool would print 10. Cadence notes this is common and convenient but not strictly correct: by definition, throughput here is 0.1 words per clock period.[2] A larger “10” means slower output, the opposite of a true rate.

Key terms · Unit 3
Definitions
Latency
Time to produce one result. Units: s, ns, clock periods.
Throughput
Results produced per unit of time, e.g. cars/h, words/s.
Memory bandwidth
Another name for a memory system’s throughput.
Design goal
Maximal throughput with acceptable latency.
Relating the two
Overlap
Many items in progress at once lets throughput exceed 1 ÷ latency.
Little’s law
Items in progress = throughput × latency (5/h × 8 h = 40 cars).
Hardware terms
Clock period
One clock tick; 10 ns at 100 MHz.

Words per clock period
